# 8. AI Governance

AI governance is the most important condition for responsible adoption. The firm should not treat AI as a general tool that every person can use in any way they choose. AI should be governed through clear rules, approved systems, access controls, human review, confidentiality safeguards, and partner oversight.

The purpose of AI governance is not to slow adoption. Its purpose is to make adoption safe, professional, and useful.

The firm should adopt a governance model based on six principles:

1. client confidentiality must be protected;
2. lawyer judgment must remain central;
3. AI outputs must be reviewed before external use;
4. legal sources must be verified;
5. AI use must be recorded where it affects legal work;
6. high-risk uses must be approved by a partner or authorized senior lawyer.

## 8.1 Approved AI Tools

The firm should maintain a list of approved AI tools. Staff should not use unapproved AI tools for client work, confidential information, privileged information, legal analysis, contracts, pleadings, arbitration documents, internal financial data, or sensitive firm information.

The approved-tool list should identify:

- tool name;
- vendor;
- permitted users;
- permitted tasks;
- prohibited tasks;
- whether client data may be used;
- whether confidential data may be used;
- whether the tool stores prompts or outputs;
- whether the vendor uses data for training;
- data-processing terms;
- security controls;
- retention settings;
- approval date;
- responsible internal owner.

The firm should separate AI tools into categories.

| Tool category | Permitted use | Main restriction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public AI tools | General non-confidential drafting, public information summaries, marketing ideas | No client names, confidential facts, privileged information, or legal advice |
| Firm-approved secure AI tools | Contract review, document summaries, due diligence, client updates, internal matter work | Only if vendor, security, and data-processing terms are approved |
| Restricted legal AI workspace | Sensitive disputes, arbitration, M&A, government-related, or privileged matters | Limited users, partner oversight, strict logging |
| Internal knowledge tools | Search and summarize approved firm precedents, templates, legal updates, checklists | Must not expose confidential material outside approved environment |

The firm should make the approved-tool list easy to access. It should also update the list when tools, vendors, security terms, or internal policies change.

## 8.2 Prohibited Uses

The firm should clearly identify what AI must not be used for. This is necessary because many AI risks arise from unclear boundaries.

AI should not be used for:

- final legal advice without lawyer review;
- final client letters without lawyer approval;
- court filings without lawyer and partner review;
- arbitration submissions without lawyer and partner review;
- legal research citations without source verification;
- confidential client documents in public AI tools;
- privileged strategy discussions in unapproved tools;
- witness statement drafting without strict lawyer control;
- generating facts, evidence, or legal authorities that do not exist;
- translating sensitive legal documents without qualified review;
- sending automated client communications without human approval;
- making final conflict-check decisions;
- making final KYC or client acceptance decisions;
- making final pricing, discount, write-off, or billing decisions;
- making employment, hiring, discipline, or performance decisions about staff without human review.

The firm should communicate these prohibited uses clearly to all lawyers and staff.

## 8.3 Confidentiality Controls

Confidentiality is central to law-firm work. The firm should apply strict controls before any client or matter information is used with AI.

The firm should classify data before AI use.

| Data type | Example | AI treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public information | Published laws, public articles, website text, public firm materials | May be used in approved ways |
| Internal non-confidential information | General templates, approved public marketing drafts, training notes | May be used in approved internal tools |
| Client confidential information | Client contracts, correspondence, transaction documents, matter facts | Use only in approved secure tools |
| Privileged information | Legal strategy, advice, dispute analysis, attorney-client communications | Use only in restricted approved workspace, if permitted |
| Highly sensitive information | Government-related matters, major disputes, M&A, investigations, arbitration strategy | Partner approval required before AI use |

The firm should require staff to ask three questions before using AI:

1. Does this information identify a client or matter?
2. Is this information confidential, privileged, or sensitive?
3. Is the AI tool approved for this type of information?

If the answer is uncertain, the staff member should not use AI until approval is obtained.

## 8.4 Human Review

Human review is not optional. AI can assist legal work, but it cannot replace professional judgment.

The firm should define review levels.

| Review level | Required for | Reviewer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic review | Internal drafts, non-legal summaries, administrative outputs | Responsible staff member or lawyer |
| Lawyer review | Client updates, legal memos, contract review, research summaries | Qualified lawyer |
| Senior lawyer review | Higher-risk advice, complex contracts, sensitive client issues | Senior lawyer or legal consultant |
| Partner review | Court filings, arbitration submissions, final legal opinions, strategic advice, high-value matters | Partner |
| Specialist review | Data protection, cybersecurity, finance, regulatory, or technical outputs | Relevant specialist or external adviser |

The firm should also require every AI-assisted external output to be checked for:

- factual accuracy;
- legal accuracy;
- source reliability;
- client instructions;
- confidentiality;
- privilege;
- tone;
- commercial context;
- formatting;
- translation meaning, where relevant.

No AI-assisted legal output should be sent externally until the required review is complete.

## 8.5 Source Verification

AI can produce incorrect legal references, incomplete summaries, and unsupported statements. For this reason, source verification should be mandatory for legal research and legal analysis.

The firm should require source verification for:

- legal opinions;
- client alerts;
- research memos;
- court filings;
- arbitration submissions;
- regulatory advice;
- employment advice;
- data protection advice;
- corporate advice;
- dispute strategy;
- legal updates;
- any document that cites laws, regulations, judgments, authorities, rules, or official guidance.

The source-verification process should confirm:

- whether the source exists;
- whether it is current;
- whether it is official or reliable;
- whether the AI summary is accurate;
- whether the cited point supports the legal conclusion;
- whether the legal position has changed;
- whether Arabic, English, or Chinese versions create translation issues.

AI should help create a source-checking list, but a lawyer must verify the sources.

## 8.6 Data Protection

The firm should ensure that AI use is consistent with data protection obligations. This is especially important where AI tools process personal data, employee data, client data, customer data, or cross-border data.

Before using AI with personal data, the firm should consider:

- what personal data is being used;
- why the data is needed;
- whether the data can be minimized or anonymized;
- whether the tool is approved for personal data;
- whether a data-processing agreement is in place;
- where the data is stored;
- whether data is transferred outside Saudi Arabia;
- whether access is limited;
- whether prompts and outputs are retained;
- whether the data may be used for model training;
- how the data can be deleted;
- whether the client has approved or needs to be informed.

The firm should apply data minimization. AI tools should receive only the information needed for the task.

For sensitive matters, anonymized or redacted information should be used where possible.

## 8.7 AI Usage Logs

The firm should maintain AI usage logs for legal and matter-related work. This creates accountability and allows the firm to review how AI is being used.

An AI usage log should include:

- date of use;
- user;
- matter number or internal reference;
- tool used;
- task type;
- input category;
- whether client data was used;
- whether confidential data was used;
- whether privileged data was used;
- output type;
- reviewer;
- source-verification status;
- final approval status;
- whether the output was used externally;

The usage log does not need to be burdensome. It should be simple enough for staff to complete quickly.

The main purpose is to create an internal record that shows AI was used responsibly.

## 8.8 AI Output Labels

The firm should label AI-assisted work internally. This will help prevent draft material from being sent before proper review.

Recommended labels include:

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| AI Draft — Not Reviewed | Output has not been checked and must not be sent externally |
| Lawyer Reviewed | A lawyer has reviewed the output for legal and factual accuracy |
| Source Verified | Legal citations, authorities, or factual sources have been checked |
| Translation Verified | Bilingual or multilingual legal meaning has been checked |
| Partner Approved | Output is approved for external use in a high-risk or final context |
| Internal Use Only | Output may be used internally but not sent to clients or third parties |
| Client Ready | Output has completed required review and approval |

These labels should be used in document names, matter folders, workflow systems, or internal checklists.

Training should cover:

- approved AI tools;

- data protection rules;

- source verification;
- review requirements;
- AI usage logs;
- output labels;
- common AI errors;
- safe use of translation tools;
- use of AI in legal research;
- use of AI in contracts;
- use of AI in disputes and arbitration;
- use of AI in client communication;
- reporting mistakes or concerns.

Training should be practical. It should use examples from the firm’s actual types of work, such as contract review, dispute chronology, PDPL assessment, legal updates, China Desk materials, and client updates.

The firm should require all lawyers and relevant staff to complete basic AI training before using approved AI tools for legal work.

# 9. Implementation Roadmap

AI implementation should be phased. The firm should not begin with complex firmwide automation. It should begin with governance, workflow mapping, training, and controlled pilot use cases.

The roadmap below is designed to move the firm from basic readiness to practical adoption and then to client-facing service improvement.

## 9.1 Phase 1: 0–30 Days — Governance and Workflow Setup

The first 30 days should focus on safe foundations.

### Objective

Prepare the firm for controlled AI use.

### Key actions

1. Appoint an internal AI implementation lead.
2. Create a small AI working group.
3. Approve initial AI governance rules.
4. Create an approved-tool list.
5. Define prohibited AI uses.
6. Create AI access levels.
7. Create a basic AI usage log.
8. Create AI output labels.
9. Identify priority workflows for pilots.
10. Train partners, lawyers, trainees, legal coordinators, assistants, finance, and business development staff.

### Priority workflows to map

The firm should map the following workflows:

- client intake;
- conflict-check information gathering;
- KYC and onboarding;
- contract review;
- legal research;
- dispute chronology;
- arbitration document management;
- client updates;
- billing narratives;
- legal updates.

Each workflow map should identify:

- current process;
- responsible person;
- documents used;
- pain points;
- risks;
- AI opportunity;
- required controls;
- expected benefit.

### Outputs by the end of Phase 1

By the end of the first 30 days, the firm should have:

- AI governance note;
- approved-tool list;
- prohibited-use list;
- access-level model;
- usage-log template;
- output-label model;
- training session materials;
- list of pilot workflows;
- responsible owners for each pilot.

This phase is about control and readiness. It is not about rapid automation.

## 9.2 Phase 2: 30–90 Days — Pilot Tools

The next 60 days should focus on practical pilots. The firm should select a small number of use cases with clear benefit and manageable risk.

### Objective

Test AI in controlled workflows and measure practical value.

### Recommended pilots

| Pilot | Owner | Main output |

### Pilot rules

Each pilot should have:

- defined scope;
- approved users;
- approved data type;
- sample matter or document set;
- review process;
- success metric;
- risk log;
- partner sponsor;
- end-of-pilot review.

The firm should not judge pilots only by speed. It should also review quality, accuracy, user adoption, client-service benefit, risk level, and ease of supervision.

### Outputs by the end of Phase 2

By the end of 90 days, the firm should have:

- pilot results summary;
- list of tools to continue;
- list of tools to stop or revise;
- updated governance rules;
- improved prompt templates;
- first set of approved AI workflows;
- initial KPI results;
- staff feedback;
- leadership decision on scaling.
